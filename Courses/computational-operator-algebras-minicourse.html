About

This course explores how operator-theoretic thinking, random matrices, and free probability can be used to regularize noisy spectra in high-dimensional covariance problems, with case studies in portfolio risk and genomic computational biology.

Advanced Mathematics + Computation + Applied Spectral Geometry

From Noncommutative Probability to Clean Spectra

This intensive mini-course introduces students to a computational view of operator algebras and free probability through one central practical problem: when data are high-dimensional and noisy, the observed spectrum of a covariance or correlation matrix is often a distorted image of the underlying signal. Students learn to simulate, visualize, clean, and interpret spectra using random matrix theory and free-probabilistic ideas, then apply those ideas to finance and genomic computational biology.

Central Thesis

Eigenvalues are not merely numerical outputs; in high-dimensional data they become a compressed representation of signal, noise, correlation, geometry, and uncertainty. Spectral regularization is the art of deciding which spectral features deserve belief.

Research Arc

Operator algebras → noncommutative probability → random matrices → Marchenko–Pastur bulk → covariance cleaning → portfolio risk and genomic signal discovery.

Design Note

This course is intentionally ambitious. It does not attempt a full graduate treatment of von Neumann algebras, C*-algebras, or free probability. Instead, it uses computational experiments to give advanced students a vivid path into modern spectral thinking: matrices as observables, trace as expectation, eigenvalue distributions as empirical laws, and spectral cleaning as a practical form of mathematical judgment.

Operators, Spectra, and Noncommutative Probability

Matrix spectra become objects of probabilistic and geometric investigation.

Day 1 — Matrices as Observables, Spectra as Data

From finite-dimensional operators to empirical spectral distributions.

Essential QuestionWhat does an eigenvalue distribution know about a matrix that individual entries hide?
0:00–0:20

Opening Demonstration: Same Entries, Different Spectra

Students compare random matrices, diagonal matrices, covariance matrices, and adjacency-like matrices with similar scale but radically different spectral shapes.

Initial conjecture: what spectral shape might mean
0:20–0:55

Mini-Lecture: Operator Algebra in Finite Dimensions

Matrices as linear operators, multiplication as noncommutative composition, adjoints, self-adjoint matrices, positivity, trace, normalized trace, and states.

Key shift: expectation becomes normalized trace, and random variables become operators.

0:55–1:30

Lab 1: Empirical Spectral Distributions

Students generate symmetric matrices, compute eigenvalues, plot histograms and kernel density estimates, and track how spectra stabilize as dimension grows.

Notebook checkpoint: spectral_distribution(A)
1:30–1:40

Break

Instructor reviews plots and helps debug eigenvalue routines.

1:40–2:20

Mini-Lecture: Moments, Traces, and Spectral Laws

Moments as normalized traces: \(\tau(A^k)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}(A^k)\). Students connect moments to spectral histograms and see why spectra can be studied through traces.

2:20–3:05

Lab 2: Wigner Matrices and the Semicircle Law

Students simulate Wigner ensembles under different scalings and observe convergence toward the semicircle distribution.

Plot: dimension vs spectral stabilization
3:05–3:35

Seminar: Representation and Compression

Discussion: what is lost and gained when a high-dimensional matrix is represented only by its spectrum?

Phenomenological theme: spectra as compressed experience of structure
3:35–4:00

Capstone Track Preview

Students choose preliminary interests: portfolio correlation cleaning, genomic PCA denoising, free convolution simulation, covariance shrinkage comparison, or spectral visualization.

Exit ticket: one application and one spectral question
2

Random Matrices and the Geometry of Noise

The Marchenko–Pastur law and the high-dimensional covariance problem.

Day 2 — Sample Covariance, Marchenko–Pastur, and Eigenvalue Cleaning

Why high-dimensional covariance matrices lie, and how to regularize them.

Essential QuestionWhen is an eigenvalue a signal, and when is it just the geometry of noise?
0:00–0:25

Warm-Up: The Curse of Estimating Correlation

Students compute sample covariance matrices when p is comparable to n and observe unstable eigenvalues.

0:25–1:05

Mini-Lecture: Wishart Matrices and Marchenko–Pastur Bulk

Sample covariance as \(S=\frac{1}{n}X^{T}X\); aspect ratio \(q=p/n\); eigenvalue spread caused by noise; bulk edges \(\lambda_{\pm}=\sigma^2(1\pm\sqrt{q})^2\); and finite-sample interpretation.

1:05–1:35

Lab 3: Simulating the MP Bulk

Students generate Gaussian data matrices, plot covariance eigenvalues, overlay theoretical bulk edges, and test how \(q=p/n\) changes the spectrum.

Notebook checkpoint: mp_edges(q, sigma2)
1:35–1:45

Break

Quick critique of plotted spectra.

1:45–2:30

Mini-Lecture: Cleaning, Shrinkage, and Regularization

Eigenvalue clipping, shrinkage toward identity, nonlinear shrinkage intuition, factor/bulk separation, and the dangers of over-interpreting principal components.

2:30–3:10

Lab 4: Spectral Cleaning Algorithms

Students implement basic eigenvalue clipping: replace eigenvalues in the Marchenko–Pastur noise bulk \([\lambda_-,\lambda_+]\) by a regularized value, then compare raw covariance, diagonal shrinkage, and bulk-averaged covariance estimators.

Notebook checkpoint: clean_covariance(S, q)
3:10–3:35

Seminar: Statistical Truth vs Spectral Appearance

Students discuss why a dominant eigenvector might be meaningful in one setting and misleading in another.

3:35–4:00

Capstone Proposal Sprint

Teams define a data source, a noise model, a regularization method, and a target comparison.

Team artifact: project claim + spectral method
3

Free Probability and Spectral Deconvolution

From independence to freeness; from observed spectra to hidden signal.

Day 3 — Freeness, Free Convolution, and Signal Recovery

How noncommutative probability helps reason about additive and multiplicative spectral noise.

Essential QuestionCan we infer the spectrum of a hidden signal from the distorted spectrum of noisy data?
0:00–0:25

Opening Experiment: Add Two Matrices

Students compare eigenvalues of \(A\), \(B\), and \(A+B\). They test why eigenvalues do not simply add entrywise or componentwise.

0:25–1:05

Mini-Lecture: From Classical Independence to Freeness

Noncommutative probability spaces, states, moments, free independence, asymptotic freeness of large random matrices, and why eigenvalue laws combine nonclassically.

1:05–1:35

Lab 5: Additive Free Convolution by Simulation

Students create deterministic diagonal signal matrices \(D\), add randomly rotated Wigner noise \(U W U^{T}\), and observe how the spectrum of \(D+U W U^{T}\) changes.

Notebook checkpoint: signal_plus_noise_spectrum
1:35–1:45

Break

Discuss common surprises from additive spectra.

1:45–2:25

Mini-Lecture: Multiplicative Noise and Sample Covariance

Sample covariance spectra as multiplicatively distorted population spectra: observed law \(\mu_S\) is treated heuristically as a free multiplicative convolution \(\mu_{\Sigma}\boxtimes \mu_{MP}\). Free deconvolution is introduced as the idea of undoing noise at the level of spectral laws.

2:25–3:05

Lab 6: Spike Detection and Deformation

Students simulate spiked covariance models \(\Sigma=I+\theta vv^{T}\) and detect when a population spike separates from the Marchenko–Pastur bulk.

Plot: spike strength vs observed outlier eigenvalue
3:05–3:30

Seminar: Inverse Problems and Epistemic Modesty

Students discuss what spectral data can justify, what it cannot justify, and why regularization requires explicit assumptions.

Theme: mathematical humility under noisy representation
3:30–4:00

Capstone Studio

Teams add one free-probability or RMT concept to their project: MP bulk, spike threshold, shrinkage comparison, spectral deconvolution idea, or factor/bulk separation.

Project checkpoint: method diagram + preliminary plot
