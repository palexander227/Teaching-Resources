Cryptography, Blockchain & Post-Quantum Security

This advanced mini-course introduces students to the mathematical foundations of modern cryptography and secure digital communication. Beginning with modular arithmetic and number theory, students build RSA, simulate blockchain systems, and investigate how quantum computing motivates post-quantum cryptography.

The course emphasizes proof-oriented reasoning, computational experimentation, and the phenomenological role of mathematical representation: students learn how symbolic, algorithmic, graphical, and network representations each reveal different aspects of secrecy, trust, and computational hardness.

Central Questions

  • How can public information produce private communication?
  • What makes a mathematical problem computationally hard?
  • Can trust be distributed without a central authority?
  • What changes when quantum computation changes the rules?

Course Arc

Number theory → modular arithmetic → RSA → hashing → blockchain → consensus → quantum threats → post-quantum cryptography.

Learning Objectives

Mathematics

Use congruences, gcd algorithms, modular exponentiation, Euler’s theorem, prime structure, and elementary algebraic reasoning to explain cryptographic systems.

Computation

Implement, test, debug, visualize, and critique cryptographic algorithms in Python or R.

Inquiry

Analyze how abstraction, representation, computational hardness, secrecy, identity, and trust interact in modern systems.

Design Note

This course is designed for advanced students who can move quickly through formal ideas but benefit from seeing how pure mathematical structures become operational systems. The goal is not merely to teach cryptographic recipes, but to cultivate a deeper sense of mathematics as a representational and infrastructural force.

1

Arithmetic, Abstraction, and Secrecy

Number theory becomes a language for secure communication.

Day 1 — Modular Arithmetic and the Birth of Encryption

From Euclid to congruence classes to fast exponentiation.

Essential QuestionHow can arithmetic wrap around itself and become a system for secrecy?
0:00–0:15

Opening Provocation: Public Rules, Private Meaning

Students receive an encrypted message and infer possible rules. Discussion introduces secrecy as public structure plus hidden information.

First conjecture log
0:15–0:45

Mini-Lecture: Divisibility, gcd, and Euclid

Divisibility, gcd, Bezout intuition, and Euclid’s algorithm as a model of efficient computation.

0:45–1:15

Guided Lab 1: Euclid in Python

Students implement iterative and recursive gcd functions, test edge cases, and compare behavior on small and large integers.

Notebook: gcd(a,b), extended_gcd(a,b)
1:15–1:25

Break

Informal debugging and instructor check-ins.

1:25–2:05

Mini-Lecture: Modular Arithmetic as Representation

Congruence, residue classes, clocks, modular inverses, and the shift from ordinary arithmetic to structured equivalence.

Phenomenological focus: line, circle, table, and alphabet as distinct representations of the same modular structure.

2:05–2:45

Guided Lab 2: Modular Calculators and Caesar Systems

Students build modular tables, implement Caesar and affine ciphers, and identify when affine decryption fails.

Notebook: affine_encrypt, affine_decrypt, invertibility test
2:45–3:25

Fast Exponentiation Studio

Students discover why repeated multiplication is too slow and implement square-and-multiply modular exponentiation.

Mini-writeup: why fast exponentiation changes feasibility
3:25–4:00

Seminar: What Makes a Problem Hard?

Students distinguish hard for humans, hard for computers, hard in general, and hard only at scale.

Exit: one structure, one computation, one question
2

Public-Key Cryptography

RSA as a meeting point of primes, exponents, inverses, and hardness.

Day 2 — RSA: Build It, Use It, Break It

Euler’s theorem, public/private keys, and the factorization assumption.

Essential QuestionHow can a lock be public while the key remains private?
0:00–0:20

Warm-Up: Modular Inverses

Students solve inverse puzzles by hand and verify with code.

0:20–1:00

Mini-Lecture: Euler, Totients, and RSA

Prime selection, n = pq, phi(n), public exponent e, private exponent d, encryption, and decryption.

Proof sketch: exponentiation cycles return information when the inverse exponent is chosen correctly.

1:00–1:35

Guided Lab 3: Toy RSA From Scratch

Students generate small primes, compute keys, encode short messages, encrypt, and decrypt.

Notebook: generate_keypair, encrypt, decrypt
1:35–1:45

Break

Instructor identifies common implementation errors.

1:45–2:25

Attack Lab: When RSA Fails

Students factor small n, test poor prime choices, recover private keys, and observe implementation vulnerabilities.

Attack notebook: factor_n, recover_private_key
2:25–3:00

Seminar: Security Assumptions

Students distinguish theorem, algorithm, implementation, and assumption.

3:00–3:35

Capstone Launch Studio

Project tracks: RSA attack, blockchain simulator, lattice visualization, quantum threat explainer, ECC concept demo, cryptographic ethics, or original proposal.

Team artifact: project claim + technical goal
3:35–4:00

Lightning Conferences

Each team clarifies scope, tool choice, risk level, and presentation format.

3

Blockchain and Distributed Trust

Hashing, ledgers, proof-of-work, and tamper evidence.

Day 3 — Hashes, Blocks, Ledgers, and Consensus

How cryptographic primitives become social and economic infrastructure.

Essential QuestionCan a community create trust without a central authority?
0:00–0:20

Opening Demonstration: Tamper-Evident Records

Instructor alters one transaction in a visible chain and students predict downstream changes.

0:20–0:55

Mini-Lecture: Hash Functions

Determinism, compression, avalanche effect, preimage resistance, collision resistance, and why hashing is not encryption.

0:55–1:30

Guided Lab 4: Hash Experiments

Students hash messages, compare small changes, search for toy collisions using truncated hashes, and visualize avalanche behavior.

Notebook: hash_demo, collision_search
1:30–1:40

Break

Reset for blockchain construction.

1:40–2:25

Build Lab: A Toy Blockchain

Students define Block objects with index, timestamp, data, previous hash, nonce, and hash.

Notebook: Block class + validate_chain
2:25–3:00

Proof-of-Work Simulation

Students implement mining and measure runtime as difficulty changes.

Graph: difficulty vs observed trials/time
3:00–3:30

Seminar: Consensus, Incentives, and Limits

Byzantine agreement, majority assumptions, energy costs, financial incentives, and mathematical vs social trust.

3:30–4:00

Capstone Studio

Teams integrate blockchain or hash ideas and prepare one system diagram.

Project checkpoint: technical diagram + next-day plan

Quantum Threats and Post-Quantum Cryptography

What changes when the computational universe changes?

Day 4 — Quantum Disruption and New Hard Problems

From Shor’s algorithm to lattice-based security.

Essential QuestionWhat mathematical structures might survive quantum computation?
0:00–0:25

Opening Contrast: Classical Search vs Quantum Speedup

Students consider how a change in computational model can turn secure assumptions into fragile assumptions.

0:25–1:05

Mini-Lecture: Why Shor Threatens RSA

Factoring becomes tractable through period finding; emphasis is on consequences for number-theoretic security.

1:05–1:35

Computational Demonstration: Period Finding Toy Models

Students explore periodic modular exponentiation tables and connect hidden periodicity to factoring.

Notebook: period_table(a,n)
1:35–1:45

Break

Transition from broken assumptions to new assumptions.

1:45–2:30

Mini-Lecture: Lattices and Geometry of Hardness

Vectors, integer combinations, lattice bases, shortest vector intuition, good and bad bases.

2:30–3:05

Guided Lab 5: Lattice Visualization

Students generate 2D lattices, plot bases, search short vectors by brute force, and compare representations.

Notebook: lattice_plot + shortest_vector_bruteforce
3:05–3:35

Post-Quantum Survey

Brief comparison of lattice-based, hash-based, code-based, and multivariate approaches.

3:35–4:00

Capstone Final Build Sprint

Teams finalize code, diagrams, claims, limitations, and presentation structure.

Skeleton: claim, demo, math, limitation, implication

Capstone Presentations II

Class maps modular arithmetic, RSA, hashing, blockchain, quantum threat, lattices, and post-quantum security.

Final Reflection

Students write: “What changed in my understanding of mathematics as infrastructure?”

Final artifact: project link + reflection paragraph
