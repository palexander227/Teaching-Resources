About

This mini-course treats cryptography as a meeting point of pure mathematics, computation, economics, trust, and civilization-scale infrastructure. Students move from modular arithmetic and number theory into RSA, blockchain, quantum threats, and post-quantum cryptography through hands-on computational labs and a final capstone presentation.

Overview

Students begin with the elementary number theory that makes modern encryption possible: divisibility, congruences, modular exponentiation, primes, and computational hardness. From there, they build and attack toy RSA systems, construct a simple blockchain, examine the mathematics of distributed trust, and investigate why quantum computing threatens current public-key systems. The course concludes with a studio-style capstone project in which students design, implement, analyze, or visualize a cryptographic system.

The deeper intellectual arc is representational: students learn how symbolic, graphical, algorithmic, and computational representations each reveal different aspects of mathematical structure. Security is treated not as magic, but as a phenomenon emerging from representation, abstraction, and computational asymmetry.

Central Questions

  • How can public information still produce private communication?
  • What makes a mathematical problem computationally hard?
  • How can trust be distributed across a network?
  • Why does quantum computation disrupt classical assumptions?
  • What mathematical structures might secure a post-quantum world?

Course Materials

  • Custom Jupyter notebooks and instructor notes
  • Python standard library: math, random, hashlib, secrets
  • Optional: sympy, numpy, matplotlib, R/tidyverse
  • Selected excerpts from Koblitz, Singh, Paar/Pelzl, Nakamoto, and NIST PQC resources

Learning Objectives

Mathematics

Use congruences, gcd algorithms, Euler’s theorem, modular exponentiation, prime structure, and elementary algebraic reasoning to explain cryptographic systems.

Computation

Implement, test, debug, visualize, and critique cryptographic procedures in Python or R using careful algorithmic reasoning.

Inquiry

Explain how abstraction, representation, computational hardness, and trust interact in modern security systems.

1

Arithmetic, Abstraction, and Secrecy

Number theory becomes a language for secure communication.

Day 1 — Modular Arithmetic and the Birth of Encryption

From Euclid to congruence classes to fast exponentiation.

Essential QuestionHow can arithmetic wrap around itself and become a system for secrecy?
0:00–0:15

Opening Provocation: Public Rules, Private Meaning

Students receive a short encrypted message and try to infer the rule. Discussion introduces the idea that secrecy can arise from a public system plus hidden structure.

Exit artifact: first conjecture log
0:15–0:45

Mini-Lecture: Divisibility, gcd, and Euclid

Review divisibility, gcd, Bezout intuition, and Euclid’s algorithm as a model of efficient computation.

Board sequence: numerical gcd → recursive algorithm → proof sketch → computational complexity intuition.

0:45–1:15

Guided Lab 1: Euclid in Python

Students implement iterative and recursive gcd functions, test edge cases, and compare execution behavior on small and large integers.

Notebook checkpoint: gcd(a,b), extended_gcd(a,b)
1:15–1:25

Break

Informal debugging and instructor check-ins.

1:25–2:05

Mini-Lecture: Modular Arithmetic as Representation

Congruence, residue classes, clocks, modular inverses, and the shift from ordinary arithmetic to structured equivalence.

Phenomenological focus: what becomes visible when the same number is represented on a line, a circle, a table, and a code alphabet?

2:05–2:45

Guided Lab 2: Modular Calculators and Caesar Systems

Students build modular addition/multiplication tables, implement Caesar and affine ciphers, and identify when affine decryption fails.

Notebook checkpoint: affine_encrypt, affine_decrypt, invertibility test
2:45–3:25

Fast Exponentiation Studio

Students discover why repeated multiplication is too slow and implement square-and-multiply modular exponentiation.

Mini-writeup: why fast exponentiation changes what is feasible
3:25–4:00

Seminar: What Makes a Problem Hard?

Students distinguish hard for humans, hard for computers, hard in general, and hard only at scale. Closing reflection connects elegance, opacity, and computational asymmetry.

Exit ticket: one mathematical structure, one computational insight, one question
2

Public-Key Cryptography

RSA as a concrete meeting point of primes, exponents, inverses, and hardness.

Day 2 — RSA: Build It, Use It, Break It

Euler’s theorem, public/private keys, and the factorization assumption.

Essential QuestionHow can a lock be public while the key remains private?
0:00–0:20

Warm-Up: Modular Inverses

Students solve inverse puzzles by hand and then verify by code. The extended Euclidean algorithm becomes an engine for decryption.

0:20–1:00

Mini-Lecture: Euler, Totients, and RSA Architecture

Prime selection, n = pq, phi(n), public exponent e, private exponent d, encryption m^e mod n, decryption c^d mod n.

Proof sketch emphasizes why exponentiation cycles return information when the right inverse exponent is used.

1:00–1:35

Guided Lab 3: Toy RSA From Scratch

Students generate small primes, compute keys, encode short messages, encrypt, and decrypt.

Notebook checkpoint: generate_keypair, encrypt, decrypt
1:35–1:45

Break

Instructor circulates and identifies common implementation errors.

1:45–2:25

Attack Lab: When RSA Fails

Students factor small n, test poor prime choices, use repeated-message attacks, and observe how implementation choices affect security.

Attack notebook: factor_n, recover_private_key
2:25–3:00

Seminar: Security Assumptions

Class distinguishes theorem, algorithm, implementation, and assumption. Students identify which parts of RSA are proven and which depend on computational limits.

3:00–3:35

Capstone Launch Studio

Students choose project tracks: RSA attack, blockchain simulator, lattice visualization, quantum threat explainer, ECC concept demo, cryptographic ethics, or original proposal.

Team artifact: project claim + minimal technical goal
3:35–4:00

Lightning Conferences

Each team meets briefly with instructor to clarify scope, tool choice, risk level, and presentation format.

3

Blockchain and Distributed Trust

Hashing, ledgers, proof-of-work, and the mathematics of tamper evidence.

Day 3 — Hashes, Blocks, Ledgers, and Consensus

How cryptographic primitives become social and economic infrastructure.

Essential QuestionCan a community create trust without a central authority?
0:00–0:20

Opening Demonstration: Tamper-Evident Records

Instructor alters one transaction in a visible chain and students predict which downstream values must change.

0:20–0:55

Mini-Lecture: Hash Functions

Determinism, compression, avalanche effect, preimage resistance, collision resistance, and why hash functions are not encryption.

0:55–1:30

Guided Lab 4: Hash Experiments

Students use hashlib to hash messages, compare small changes, search for toy collisions using truncated hashes, and visualize avalanche behavior.

Notebook checkpoint: hash_demo, collision_search
1:30–1:40

Break

Reset for blockchain construction.

1:40–2:25

Build Lab: A Toy Blockchain

Students define Block objects with index, timestamp, data, previous hash, nonce, and hash. They add validation rules and test tampering.

Notebook checkpoint: Block class + validate_chain
2:25–3:00

Proof-of-Work Simulation

Students implement mining by searching for hashes with a fixed prefix, then vary difficulty and measure runtime.

Graph: difficulty vs observed trials/time
3:00–3:30

Seminar: Consensus, Incentives, and Limits

Discussion of Byzantine agreement, majority assumptions, energy costs, financial incentives, and the difference between mathematical trust and social trust.

3:30–4:00

Capstone Studio

Teams integrate blockchain/hash ideas where relevant, update project notebooks, and prepare one diagram explaining their system.

Project checkpoint: one technical diagram + next-day plan
4

Quantum Threats and Post-Quantum Cryptography

What changes when the computational universe changes?

Day 4 — Quantum Disruption and New Hard Problems

From Shor’s algorithm to lattice-based security.

Essential QuestionWhat mathematical structures might survive quantum computation?
0:00–0:25

Opening Contrast: Classical Search vs Quantum Speedup

Conceptual demonstration of how a change in computational model can turn secure assumptions into fragile assumptions.

0:25–1:05

Mini-Lecture: Why Shor Threatens RSA

Students learn the high-level structure: factoring becomes tractable through period finding. No quantum mechanics prerequisite; emphasis is on the consequences for number-theoretic security.

1:05–1:35

Computational Demonstration: Period Finding Toy Models

Students explore periodic modular exponentiation tables and see how hidden periodicity connects to factoring.

Notebook checkpoint: period_table(a,n)
1:35–1:45

Break

Transition from broken assumptions to new assumptions.

1:45–2:30

Mini-Lecture: Lattices and Geometry of Hardness

Vectors, integer combinations, lattice bases, shortest vector intuition, good and bad bases, and why geometry can support post-quantum cryptography.

2:30–3:05

Guided Lab 5: Lattice Visualization

Students generate 2D lattices, plot bases, search short vectors by brute force, and compare how representation affects difficulty.

Notebook checkpoint: lattice_plot + shortest_vector_bruteforce
3:05–3:35

Post-Quantum Survey

Brief comparison of lattice-based, hash-based, code-based, and multivariate approaches. Students identify which hard problem each system relies on.

3:35–4:00

Capstone Final Build Sprint

Teams finalize code, diagrams, claims, limitations, and presentation structure.

Presentation skeleton due: claim, demo, math, limitation, implication
5

Capstone Symposium

Students present a working artifact and defend the mathematical idea behind it.

Day 5 — Exhibition of Cryptographic Inquiry

Presentation, critique, and synthesis.

Essential QuestionWhat does your project reveal about security, representation, and trust?
0:00–0:20

Presentation Warm-Up

Teams verify demos, test notebooks, and rehearse the core claim in one minute.

0:20–1:50

Capstone Presentations I

Each team presents a 7-minute demo followed by 3 minutes of technical questions.

Required: mathematical explanation + computational artifact
1:50–2:00

Break

Peer feedback cards collected and sorted.

2:00–3:10

Capstone Presentations II

Remaining teams present. Audience members ask questions about assumptions, limitations, and possible extensions.

3:10–3:35

Synthesis Seminar

Class maps the week’s concepts: modular arithmetic, RSA, hashing, blockchain, quantum threat, lattices, and post-quantum security.

3:35–4:00

Final Reflection

Students write a brief reflection: “What changed in my understanding of mathematics as infrastructure?”

Final artifact: project link + reflection paragraph

Instructor Design Note

This mini-course is designed for advanced students who can move quickly through formal ideas but benefit from seeing how pure mathematical structures become operational systems. The aim is not merely to teach cryptographic recipes, but to cultivate a deeper sense of mathematics as a representational and infrastructural force: a way of organizing trust, secrecy, identity, and computation in the modern world.
