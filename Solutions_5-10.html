Advanced Precalculus
Solutions to Problems 5–10

Problem 5

Let $f$ be a polynomial satisfying $$ f(1)=2,\quad f(2)=5,\quad f(4)=17. $$ When $f(x)$ is divided by $(x-1)(x-2)(x-4)$, the remainder is $$ R(x)=ax^2+bx+c. $$

(a) Determine $R(x)$

By the Remainder Theorem, $$ R(1)=2,\quad R(2)=5,\quad R(4)=17. $$ Thus: $$ \begin{cases} a+b+c=2\\ 4a+2b+c=5\\ 16a+4b+c=17 \end{cases} $$ Subtracting gives: $$ 3a+b=3,\quad 6a+b=6. $$ Hence $a=1$, $b=0$, $c=1$.

$$ R(x)=x^2+1 $$

(b) Why the result does not depend on the degree of $f$

The remainder depends only on the values of $f$ at the roots of the divisor. Any higher-degree terms are absorbed into the quotient and vanish at $x=1,2,4$.

(c) Compute $R'(2)$

$$ R'(x)=2x \quad\Rightarrow\quad R'(2)=4. $$

Problem 6

Let $$ F(x)=\sqrt{x^2+ax+b}-\sqrt{x^2+cx+d}. $$

(a) Existence of $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}F(x)$

As $x\to+\infty$, $$ \sqrt{x^2+ax+b} = x\sqrt{1+\frac{a}{x}+\frac{b}{x^2}} = x+\frac{a}{2}+O\!\left(\frac1x\right), $$ $$ \sqrt{x^2+cx+d} = x+\frac{c}{2}+O\!\left(\frac1x\right). $$ Thus the limit always exists.

(b) Value of the limit as $x\to+\infty$

$$ \lim_{x\to+\infty}F(x)=\frac{a-c}{2}. $$

(c) Behavior as $x\to-\infty$

Since $\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$, $$ \sqrt{x^2+ax+b}=-x-\frac{a}{2}+O\!\left(\frac1x\right), $$ $$ \sqrt{x^2+cx+d}=-x-\frac{c}{2}+O\!\left(\frac1x\right). $$

$$ \lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=\frac{c-a}{2}. $$

The limits differ because $|x|=x$ for $x\to+\infty$ but $|x|=-x$ for $x\to-\infty$.

Problem 7

Suppose $f$ is a polynomial such that $$ \lim_{x\to3}\frac{f(x)}{(x-3)^2}=7. $$

(a) Multiplicity

The finiteness of the limit implies $$ f(x)=(x-3)^2g(x), $$ with $g(3)=7\neq0$. Thus $x=3$ is a root of multiplicity at least $2$.

(b) Limit involving $f'(x)$

Differentiate: $$ f'(x)=2(x-3)g(x)+(x-3)^2g'(x). $$ Divide by $(x-3)$ and take the limit:

$$ \lim_{x\to3}\frac{f'(x)}{x-3}=2g(3)=14. $$

(c) Why this is algebraic

All conclusions follow from polynomial factorization and differentiation, not analytic assumptions.

Problem 9

Let $$ g(x)=\sqrt{x^2+ax},\qquad h(x)=|x|+\frac{a}{2}. $$

(a) Limits of $g(x)-h(x)$

As $x\to+\infty$, $$ g(x)=x+\frac{a}{2}+O\!\left(\frac1x\right), $$ so

$$ \lim_{x\to+\infty}(g(x)-h(x))=0. $$

As $x\to-\infty$, $$ g(x)=-x-\frac{a}{2}+O\!\left(\frac1x\right), $$ so

$$ \lim_{x\to-\infty}(g(x)-h(x))=-a. $$

(b) Dominant behavior

$h(x)$ captures the leading $|x|$ growth of $g(x)$, but only matches the full asymptotic expansion on the right unless $a=0$.

(c) Geometry

$g(x)$ has asymptotes $$ y=x+\frac{a}{2}\quad(x\to+\infty),\qquad y=-x-\frac{a}{2}\quad(x\to-\infty), $$ while $h(x)$ shifts the left arm upward by $a$.

Problem 10

Let $f$ be a polynomial such that $$ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{(x-a)^n}=L, \quad L\neq0. $$

(a) Exact multiplicity — fully linearized proof

Since the limit exists and is finite, the quotient is bounded near $a$: there exist $M>0$ and $\delta>0$ such that $$ 0<|x-a|<\delta \;\Rightarrow\; \left|\frac{f(x)}{(x-a)^n}\right|\le M. $$

Rewrite: $$ f(x)=\left(\frac{f(x)}{(x-a)^n}\right)(x-a)^n. $$ Thus, $$ |f(x)|\le M|x-a|^n. $$

Since $|x-a|^n\to0$, the Squeeze Theorem gives $$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=0. $$ By continuity of polynomials, $f(a)=0$.

If the multiplicity were less than $n$, the quotient would diverge. If the multiplicity were greater than $n$, the quotient would tend to $0$. Both contradict the hypothesis.

$$ f(x)=(x-a)^n g(x),\quad g(a)=L\neq0. $$

(b) Relation to the Factor Theorem

This is the $n$th-order version of the Factor Theorem, recovered when $n=1$.

(c) Local shape

Near $x=a$, $$ f(x)\approx L(x-a)^n. $$ Parity of $n$ determines crossing vs. touching, and the sign of $L$ determines orientation.
